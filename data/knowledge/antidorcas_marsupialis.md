# Antidorcas marsupialis

**Common Name:** Springbok

**Scientific Name:** *Antidorcas marsupialis*

## Conservation Status

**Least Concern (LC)** according to the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List. Springbok populations are widespread and stable across southern Africa, with many occurring in protected areas and private reserves.

## Habitat

Springboks inhabit open grasslands, savannas, shrublands, and semi-arid regions, particularly the Karoo, Kalahari, and Namib Desert ecosystems. They are well adapted to dry environments and can survive for extended periods without direct access to water by obtaining moisture from the vegetation they consume.

## Behaviour

Springboks are highly social antelopes that usually live in herds, although herd size varies depending on the season and availability of food. During the breeding season, dominant males establish territories and compete for access to females. Springboks are famous for a distinctive behaviour known as **pronking**, in which they leap high into the air with stiff legs and an arched back, possibly to signal fitness to predators or alert other members of the herd.

## Diet

Springboks are herbivores with a flexible feeding strategy. They graze on grasses when available but also browse on leaves, shrubs, flowers, and succulents during dry periods. This adaptability allows them to survive in environments where food availability changes throughout the year.

## Ecological Importance

As abundant grazing and browsing herbivores, springboks help maintain grassland ecosystems by controlling vegetation growth. They are also an important prey species for predators such as cheetahs, leopards, lions, African wild dogs, and spotted hyenas, making them a key component of African savanna food webs.

## Major Threats

- Habitat fragmentation
- Competition with livestock in some regions
- Fencing that restricts seasonal movements
- Local hunting pressure

## Interesting Fact

The springbok gets its name from the Afrikaans words *spring* ("jump") and *bok* ("antelope"). During pronking displays, individuals can leap over 3 metres (10 feet) into the air while keeping all four legs stiff, making them one of Africa's most recognizable antelopes.
